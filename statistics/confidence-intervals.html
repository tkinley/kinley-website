Confidence intervals and the Wilson interval

A point estimate gives one number. An interval shows the precision of an estimation procedure under specified assumptions. It is useful to read the estimate and interval together.

Numerical examples on this page are hypothetical unless a worked case study is explicitly linked.

What 95% confidence means

A 95% confidence procedure is designed to contain the fixed target parameter in about 95% of repeated samples when its assumptions hold. For the interval already calculated, frequentist confidence is not a posterior probability that the parameter lies inside it.

The interval describes uncertainty addressed by that procedure. It does not automatically account for biased sampling, a post hoc subgroup search, inaccurate measurements, or unreported analyses. More observations can narrow an interval around an estimate that still has bias.

Why use Wilson for a proportion?

The simple Wald interval p̂ ± 1.96√[p̂(1−p̂)/n] can behave poorly with small samples or proportions near 0 or 1. Wilson inverts a score test and keeps limits within the allowable probability range. Its coverage is approximate, not exact for every n and p.

With z = 1.96 for a two-sided 95% interval and d = 1 + z²/n:

center = [p̂ + z²/(2n)] / d
half-width = z √[p̂(1−p̂)/n + z²/(4n²)] / d
interval = center ± half-width

Example: 12 of 20 patients respond

The observed rate is 60%. The 95% Wilson interval is about 38.7%–78.1%. That wide range reflects the limited precision of twenty observations under a binomial model.

An exact binomial interval is another option and can be conservative. Bootstrap intervals resample observed data and need a resampling scheme appropriate for the design; they do not create information absent from the sample.

Three intervals, three targets

Do not interchange these intervals
IntervalTargetInterpretation depends on
Confidence intervalAn unknown fixed population parameterA repeated-sampling procedure and its assumptions
Bayesian credible intervalA parameter with a posterior distributionThe prior, likelihood, and model
Prediction intervalA future observation or countFuture variability and the chosen prediction model

A Wilson interval does not supply a probability distribution for p. Using its endpoints as separate prediction scenarios is a sensitivity exercise, not a calibrated prediction interval.

Read intervals on the correct scale

No difference corresponds to 0 for a mean or risk difference, and 1 for a risk ratio, odds ratio, or hazard ratio. In compatible two-sided tests and intervals, excluding the null value corresponds to rejection at the matching significance level. Adjustments, methods, and prespecified thresholds must match.

Overlapping confidence intervals for two separate groups do not settle whether their difference is statistically significant. Estimate the contrast directly. Also ask whether the interval excludes effects too small to matter clinically, rather than only whether it excludes zero.
