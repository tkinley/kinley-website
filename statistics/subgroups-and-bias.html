Subgroups, enrichment, and selection bias

A treatment may work differently across patients, but finding a high response rate in a subgroup is only the beginning. The definition, search process, comparison, and independent evidence determine how much to trust it.

Numerical examples on this page are hypothetical unless a worked case study is explicitly linked.

Learn by changing the example

Select the best-looking subgroup

All hypothetical groups have true response probability 30%. Compare a group chosen in advance with the group selected for the highest observed rate.

The bars average 600 seeded simulations of independent groups. This invented search illustrates selection optimism; it does not estimate bias in any real company’s analysis. Both groups have the same true underlying p = 30%.

A correct description can be an optimistic forecast

The observed rate in a chosen subgroup can accurately describe those patients. Bias becomes a concern when a subgroup selected for looking good is used to estimate future performance without accounting for the selection process. Searching many biomarker cutoffs, combinations, or exclusions can favor an unusually positive result.

Example: the best of eight groups

Imagine eight small groups with identical true treatment-response probabilities. Random variation gives them different observed rates. Selecting the highest one after seeing outcomes makes the selected estimate optimistic on average. The exact bias depends on how the search was conducted.

A confidence interval calculated as though the selected subgroup were fixed in advance generally does not correct this selection.

Prognostic is different from predictive

A prognostic biomarker is associated with outcomes regardless of which treatment is assigned. A predictive biomarker identifies differences in treatment effect. Better outcomes in biomarker-positive patients on a single treatment may reflect prognosis rather than a greater incremental benefit from that treatment.

A randomized treatment-by-biomarker interaction analysis can address effect modification on the specified scale. “Significant in subgroup A” and “not significant in subgroup B” is not itself a significant difference between subgroup effects.

Enrichment changes who the evidence applies to

Enrichment deliberately selects patients to make a trial more informative, for example by reducing measurement variability, increasing baseline event risk, or targeting people more likely to benefit. A rule with biological rationale can improve a future design, but its performance should be evaluated prospectively.

Restricting eligibility also narrows the population to which the result directly applies. A stronger effect in a smaller selected population does not imply the same effect in all patients, or that the fraction meeting the criteria is known precisely.

Other biases survive large samples

Confounding arises when treatment or group membership is associated with other causes of outcomes. Attrition can favor one treatment if missingness depends on outcomes. Measurement bias can enter through unblinded assessment. Publication and reporting bias can make the available evidence look more favorable than the full record.

Randomization, blinding, complete follow-up, prespecification, and transparent reporting address different problems; no one method fixes all of them.

What would strengthen the finding?

  • A subgroup rule and analysis plan fixed before new outcomes are examined.
  • A justified interaction analysis when claiming differential treatment effect.
  • Independent data with comparable treatment and assessment.
  • Reporting of the full subgroup search, uncertainty, and missing data.
