Superiority, noninferiority, and equivalence

Some trials aim to show a treatment is better. Others ask whether it avoids losing more than a justified amount of efficacy, perhaps in exchange for another advantage. These need different hypotheses.

Numerical examples on this page are hypothetical unless a worked case study is explicitly linked.

Three distinct questions

Superiority asks whether a treatment improves the endpoint relative to control. Noninferiority asks whether a possible loss is smaller than a prespecified margin. Equivalence asks whether the difference lies within both a lower and an upper margin.

Failure to show superiority is not evidence of equivalence or noninferiority. An imprecise trial can be compatible with substantial benefit and substantial harm at once.

A margin needs a direction and a scale

Example: response-rate difference

Let Δ = pnew − pcontrol, so higher is better. Suppose the justified noninferiority margin is −10 percentage points. An estimated difference of −2 points with a matching 95% interval of −7 to +3 clears that margin because the lower limit is above −10.

An interval of −13 to +3 would not clear it. Both intervals include zero, so neither establishes superiority using the corresponding two-sided interval criterion.

The numerical example illustrates a conventional one-sided 2.5% test matched to a two-sided 95% interval. The actual significance level, test, endpoint scale, and multiplicity plan must be prespecified.

A margin is not a convenience setting

The margin needs clinical and statistical justification, including credible evidence about the active control’s effect and how much of that benefit must be preserved. A very wide margin can make noninferiority easier to claim while allowing a clinically important loss.

Assay sensitivity is the ability of the trial to distinguish effective from ineffective treatment. If poor adherence, weak outcome ascertainment, or an unsuitable population makes both arms look similar, a noninferiority conclusion can be misleading. The assumption that the control retains its historical effect also matters.

Analysis populations and equivalence

Intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses can be affected differently by nonadherence and exclusions. Neither should be assumed universally conservative in noninferiority trials; concordance and sensitivity need interpretation.

Equivalence requires both bounds to fall within justified margins, using the intervals and error levels matched to the testing procedure. Switching from a failed superiority test to an unplanned similarity claim changes the question after seeing the answer.

Read the advantage as well as the efficacy margin

A drug that is noninferior on one endpoint may still differ in safety, dosing burden, quality of life, or other outcomes. Those advantages need their own evidence and, where applicable, a multiplicity plan. Noninferiority is not a statement that the products are identical.
