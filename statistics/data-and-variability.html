Data types, averages, and variability

The same word, “improvement,” can refer to a responder count, a change in a laboratory value, or a delay in disease progression. These are different kinds of data and need different summaries.

Numerical examples on this page are hypothetical unless a worked case study is explicitly linked.

Learn by changing the example

Separate patient variability from estimate precision

Increase the sample size while holding individual variability fixed, then change the variability itself.

The mean is fixed at 50 units. The first span is mean ± one SD, not a 95% interval for individuals. The second is a normal-approximation 95% interval for the mean, using the chosen SD and independent-observation assumption.

Match the summary to the data

Binary data record outcomes such as response or no response. Continuous data measure quantities such as blood pressure or symptom score. Ordinal data have ordered categories whose distances need not be equal. Counts record numbers of events. Time-to-event data record how long until an event, often with incomplete follow-up.

A binomial model describes a fixed number of independent binary observations with a common probability. A normal model can approximate some continuous summaries. A Poisson model describes counts under restrictive rate assumptions; excess variability may call for another model. Choosing a distribution is an assumption to assess, not a property guaranteed by the endpoint’s name.

Mean, median, and spread answer different questions

The mean is the sum divided by the number observed. The median is the middle value. A few extreme values can move the mean more than the median. The standard deviation (SD) describes variation among individual observations; the interquartile range describes the middle half.

Example: uneven drug exposure

Five exposure values are 1, 2, 2, 3, and 12 units. Their mean is 4, while the median is 2. Reporting only the mean hides the long upper tail. Neither summary establishes which exposure is effective or safe.

A percentage change, an absolute change, and a change from baseline adjusted for baseline covariates also answer different questions. Check the units and the analysis model.

Standard error measures uncertainty in an estimate

For independent observations with common finite variance, the estimated standard error of a sample mean is SE = SD / √n. It describes how much the estimated mean would vary across repeated samples. It does not describe how much individual patients vary.

Four times as many observations halves this SE under these assumptions. Correlated observations, such as repeated visits from the same patient, cannot simply be counted as independent patients.

Adjusted means and repeated measurements

Regression can estimate a treatment contrast while accounting for prespecified baseline variables. An adjusted mean is model-based and can differ from the raw average. A mixed model for repeated measures accounts for correlations across visits and requires assumptions about the outcome and missingness.

Ask whether error bars show SD, SE, or a confidence interval; whether the analysis uses individuals or repeated measurements; and whether the displayed summary matches the primary analysis. A narrow SE bar is not evidence that every patient had a similar outcome.
