Effect sizes: absolute and relative benefit

An effect size describes the magnitude of a difference or association. Its scale determines what a headline means. Statistical significance alone does not tell us how large or useful an effect is.

Numerical examples on this page are hypothetical unless a worked case study is explicitly linked.

Example: a randomized response comparison

At the same follow-up, 40/100 patients respond on Drug A and 25/100 respond on control. Assume comparable assessment and no missing outcomes for this illustration.

Different summaries of the same hypothetical result
MeasureCalculationResult
Risk difference0.40 − 0.250.15, or 15 percentage points
Risk ratio0.40 / 0.251.60, or a 60% relative increase in response
Odds ratio(0.40/0.60) / (0.25/0.75)2.00
Number needed to treat for one additional response1 / 0.15About 7, using this point estimate and follow-up

An odds ratio of 2 does not mean the response probability doubled: the risk ratio here is 1.6. Odds and risks become closer when events are rare. Describe whether the event is beneficial or harmful before interpreting a ratio above or below 1.

An observed benefit needs an uncertainty interval

For this example, a simple unadjusted large-sample interval for the risk difference is approximately 2.2–27.8 percentage points. This calculation is illustrative; score-based or other methods may be preferable depending on sample size and design.

SE(RD) ≈ √[p̂A(1−p̂A)/nA + p̂C(1−p̂C)/nC]

The point estimate of NNT hides this uncertainty and depends on baseline risk, outcome definition, and time horizon. If the risk-difference interval includes zero, the corresponding NNT uncertainty is especially awkward and should not be reduced to a single neat range.

Continuous outcomes need units

A mean difference of −5 mmHg is an absolute blood-pressure contrast. A standardized mean difference divides by a standard deviation and is unitless, which can help compare scales but is less directly clinical. A percent change depends on the baseline; it is not interchangeable with an absolute change.

A prespecified regression model may adjust for baseline measurements or other prognostic variables. Distinguish the adjusted treatment contrast from raw group means. Statistical adjustment cannot guarantee that two unrelated studies become comparable.

Clinical importance is a separate question

A small effect can be precisely estimated in a very large trial. A potentially meaningful effect can be imprecise in a small trial. Judge the magnitude, interval, symptom or survival consequences, treatment burden, and safety together.

When reading a company release, identify the measure, direction, units, reference group, follow-up, and confidence interval. A relative percentage without the absolute outcomes omits information needed to judge its practical size.
