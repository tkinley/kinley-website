Bayesian updating and future prediction

Bayesian analysis represents uncertainty about unknown quantities with probability distributions. Updating is explicit about the data model and the information supplied before seeing the current data.

Numerical examples on this page are hypothetical unless a worked case study is explicitly linked.

Learn by changing the example

Update a prior with observed responses

Change the observed counts and prior, then compare the prior distribution with the posterior.

An independent binomial likelihood updates the selected beta prior. The priors are illustrative, not calibrated to a therapy. Credible intervals are conditional on this prior and likelihood; selection bias and population shifts are not automatically included.

Prior × likelihood gives the posterior

The prior represents information or assumptions about a parameter before the current data. The likelihood describes how those data would arise at different parameter values. The posterior combines them and is normalized to integrate to one.

posterior(p | data) ∝ likelihood(data | p) × prior(p)

A 95% posterior credible interval contains 95% of the posterior probability under that model. Its meaning differs from the repeated-sampling interpretation of a confidence interval. Neither interval automatically represents uncertainty from an unmodeled selection process.

A binary-response example

Example: 12 responses among 20 patients

Use a Beta(1,1) prior for p and an independent binomial likelihood. The posterior is Beta(13,9): add twelve responses to the first parameter and eight nonresponses to the second. The posterior mean is 13/22 ≈ 59.1%, compared with the observed proportion of 60%.

For a Beta(a,b) distribution, the mean is a/(a+b). Its concentration affects how strongly the prior influences updating. Calling a prior “weak” should be justified in the specific setting; a uniform prior on p is not uniform after every transformation.

Prediction includes future sampling variability

To predict m future patients, average Binomial(m,p) predictions over the posterior for p. A beta posterior produces a beta-binomial predictive distribution.

P(Xfuture = k | data) = ∫ P(Xfuture = k | p) π(p | data) dp

For the example’s Beta(13,9) posterior and m = 30, the expected count is 30 × 13/22 ≈ 17.7 responders. A fixed-p binomial prediction at p̂ = 0.60 has expected count 18 and ignores uncertainty about p. Their predictive spreads differ even when their means are similar.

A predictive probability of meeting a future responder threshold is conditional on the endpoint, future population, prior, and model. It is not automatically a probability of trial success, regulatory approval, or a market return.

Check the prior and the data-generating assumptions

Repeat the analysis with defensible alternative priors and report what changes. External data need a comparability argument before they are borrowed: different eligibility, measurement, background care, or follow-up can make historical information misleading.

A model fit to a retrospectively selected subgroup will not remove selection optimism merely because it is Bayesian. Calibration studies or simulations should evaluate a planned decision rule’s behavior across relevant scenarios.

What to ask about a Bayesian result

  • What is the prior, and where did its information come from?
  • What likelihood and population assumptions were made?
  • Is the reported probability about a parameter, a future outcome, or a decision rule?
  • How sensitive is it to the prior and historical borrowing?
