Interim analyses and adaptive designs

Learning during a trial can improve decisions, but repeatedly looking at accumulating results changes the statistical procedure. A valid design plans how interim information may affect the trial.

Numerical examples on this page are hypothetical unless a worked case study is explicitly linked.

Repeated significance testing creates extra opportunities

Testing at the usual final threshold after every look can increase false positives. The interim results are correlated, so treating those looks as independent tests is generally incorrect, but ignoring them is also incorrect.

A group-sequential design uses prespecified boundaries for interim and final analyses. An alpha-spending approach allocates the error budget across information times. Early efficacy boundaries are often more demanding than the final boundary, depending on the chosen design.

Efficacy, futility, and safety are different decisions

An efficacy rule stops for sufficiently strong evidence of benefit under the design. A futility rule stops when continued success appears unlikely according to a specified criterion. A safety stop responds to concerning risk and may not be based on the efficacy testing boundary.

Example: three planned looks

A trial plans analyses at 50%, 75%, and 100% of its target information. A nominal p = 0.03 at the first look might not cross its prespecified efficacy boundary, even though it is below 0.05. The actual boundaries come from the design, not this example.

Stopping for futility does not prove the treatment effect is exactly zero. A trial stopped early for a favorable effect can also have an upward-biased naive effect estimate; estimation and intervals should reflect the stopping design where appropriate.

Adaptation should be planned and evaluated

Adaptive designs can change features such as sample size, allocation, or which doses continue, according to prespecified rules using accumulating data. Blinded sample-size re-estimation based on nuisance quantities differs from changing sample size after seeing the treatment difference.

Choosing the best dose or population and then analyzing it as if no selection occurred can distort error rates and effect estimates. Simulations can evaluate operating characteristics across scenarios: false positives, power, bias, expected sample size, and stopping probabilities.

Who sees the interim data matters

An independent data monitoring committee may review unblinded information under a defined charter. Preserving trial integrity limits the ways knowledge of interim results could influence enrollment, treatment, or assessment. A company’s public update does not necessarily describe all committee analyses.

Bayesian predictive probabilities can inform an adaptive decision rule, but the rule’s behavior still needs evaluation. “Bayesian” does not mean that repeated looks, selection, or operational bias disappear.

Questions for an interim announcement

  • Was the look planned, and what fraction of information was available?
  • What boundary or predictive criterion applied?
  • Was stopping for benefit, futility, safety, or another reason?
  • Which adaptations were allowed, and who saw unblinded results?
  • Are reported estimates and intervals appropriate after selection or stopping?
