Cox regression: where a hazard ratio comes from

A press release reports a hazard ratio of 0.58. That number is not a ratio of two percentages. It is eβ, where β is the coefficient that best explains which patients had events, when they had them, and who else was still being followed at those moments.

Examples on this page use invented numbers.

Dividing percentages does not give a hazard ratio

A hazard is an instantaneous event rate among patients who are still event-free. A comparison such as “18% versus 29% had an event” ignores how long each patient was followed: someone randomized last month has had little time to have an event. A hazard ratio compares rates at the same moment, among the patients still at risk.

The simplest rate comparison counts events per unit of follow-up:

The ratio 0.058 / 0.100 = 0.58 is an incidence-rate ratio. It equals the hazard ratio only if each arm’s hazard is constant over time. Cox regression drops that assumption: it estimates the ratio while leaving the shape of the hazard over time unspecified.

The model: an unknown baseline times a multiplier

For a patient with covariate x, the Cox model writes the hazard at time t as

h(t | x) = h0(t) × eβx

h0(t) is the baseline hazard. It can have any shape, rising, falling, or uneven, and it is never estimated when finding β. Code treatment as x = 1 and control as x = 0. The control hazard is h0(t), the treatment hazard is h0(t)eβ, and their ratio is

HR = eβ, so β = ln(HR)

The baseline cancels, so the ratio is the same at every time. That is the proportional-hazards assumption. A reported HR of 0.58 corresponds to β = ln(0.58) ≈ −0.545. With several covariates the multiplier becomes eβ1x1 + β2x2 + …, and each eβk is the hazard ratio for a one-unit change in xk with the other covariates held fixed.

Each event becomes a probability over its risk set

The risk set R(t) is everyone still being followed and event-free just before time t. Suppose exactly one event happens at t. Under the model, the probability that it happened to patient i rather than to anyone else in the risk set is

P(patient i | one event in R(t)) = eβxi / Σj ∈ R(t) eβxj

The baseline h0(t) appears in every term and cancels. Multiplying these probabilities over all event times gives the partial likelihood L(β), a function of β alone. Cox regression picks the β that makes the observed order of events most probable.

Censoring enters through the risk sets. A patient censored at month 12 sits in the denominator of every event before month 12, then leaves. The model uses that event-free follow-up and assumes nothing about what happened afterward, but it does need censoring to be noninformative.

Write z = eβ for the hazard ratio. Control patients get weight 1 and treated patients weight z.

  • Month 3. All four are at risk, with weights 1, z, 1, z. The probability that the event is A’s is 1 / (2 + 2z).
  • Month 8. A has left. B, C, and D remain, with weights z, 1, z. The probability that the event is B’s is z / (1 + 2z).
L(z) = 1 / (2 + 2z) × z / (1 + 2z)

Set the derivative of ln L to zero: 1/z − 1/(1 + z) − 2/(1 + 2z) = 0, which simplifies to 1 − 2z² = 0. The estimate is z = 1/√2 ≈ 0.707, so β̂ = −½ ln 2 ≈ −0.347.

Each arm had one event, yet the estimate is below 1. At month 3, equal hazards would give the treated arm a one-in-two chance of the event, and it went to a control patient. At month 8, equal hazards would give the treated arm a two-in-three chance, and it went to a treated patient. The first surprise is bigger than the second, so the estimate falls below 1: timing, not the count. With four patients the 95% interval runs from about 0.04 to 11.8. The lab above starts with this example.

Real trials: maximize the log-likelihood, then read its curvature

With hundreds of patients there is no closed form. Software maximizes the log partial likelihood

ℓ(β) = Σevents [ βxi − ln Σj ∈ R(ti) eβxj ]

Its slope, the score U(β), is a sum of observed minus expected: at each event, xi minus the weighted average of x in the risk set. For a 0/1 treatment that is “was this event in the treated arm?” minus “how likely was that, given who was at risk and the current β?”. Its curvature, the information I(β), sums the weighted variance of x across risk sets. Newton’s method updates β ← β + U(β)/I(β); because ℓ is concave, a few steps usually converge.

The curvature also measures uncertainty: SE(β̂) = 1/√I(β̂), and the usual 95% interval is exp(β̂ ± 1.96 × SE).

Example: 110 versus 140 events

Suppose a trial has 110 events in the treatment arm and 140 in the control arm, with β̂ = −0.545 (HR 0.58) and SE 0.13. The interval is exp(−0.545 ± 0.255), about 0.45 to 0.75. A common approximation, SE ≈ √(1/110 + 1/140) ≈ 0.127, shows why: precision comes from events, not from the number of patients enrolled.

The log-rank test is the same score at β = 0

At β = 0 every weight is 1, so the expected share at each event is just the treated fraction of the risk set. Summed over events, U(0) is observed minus expected treated events, O − E, and I(0) is its variance. With no tied event times,

χ²log-rank = U(0)² / I(0)

So the log-rank test is the Cox score test at HR = 1. A trial usually reports the log-rank p-value with the Cox HR and its Wald interval. They come from the same risk sets but are different statistics, so near a significance threshold they can disagree slightly. With tied event times the two also differ a little, depending on how ties are handled. A stratified log-rank test pairs with a stratified Cox model.

Tied times, strata, and covariates

When several events share a recorded time, their exact order is unknown. Breslow’s approximation treats each tied event as if it faced the full risk set. Efron’s approximation shrinks the denominator step by step, as if the tied patients left in an averaged order, and is closer to the exact calculation when ties are common. Protocols often prespecify Efron.

A stratified Cox model gives each stratum, for example age group by disease stage, its own baseline hazard but one shared β. Risk sets are formed within strata and the partial likelihoods are multiplied. This respects how randomization was stratified without assuming the strata share a hazard shape.

Adding covariates changes the question as well as the precision. Hazard ratios are non-collapsible: even in a randomized trial with no confounding, an HR adjusted for a prognostic factor can differ from the unadjusted HR, because the two average over different groups. Check which model produced a reported number.

When the hazard ratio changes over time

Suppose the true ratio is about 1 for the first six months and 0.45 afterward. A single Cox β then estimates a kind of average over event times, weighted toward times with many events and balanced risk sets. That average depends on follow-up: an early analysis is dominated by early events, a later one by later events.

Checks include plotting ln(−ln S(t)) for each arm, which should run roughly parallel under proportional hazards; testing Schoenfeld residuals against time; and fitting a piecewise HR or a time-varying coefficient β(t). Period-specific HRs with wide intervals do not by themselves prove that the assumption failed. When hazards clearly change, restricted mean survival time or landmark survival can describe the benefit more directly.

Why not Fisher’s exact test or chi-square?

Those tests compare the proportion with an event in a 2×2 table. They ignore when events happened and treat a patient followed for 3 months like one followed for 30. Two datasets can have identical event counts and very different timing. In the lab, the “treated events earlier” data keep the same 5 and 2 events, yet the Cox estimate moves from 0.22 to 0.38 and the log-rank p-value from 0.056 to 0.23. A 2×2 table sees no change.

A crude event proportion at the data cutoff can still be reported. It depends on how long patients were followed and is not a substitute for the prespecified time-to-event analysis.

A quick consistency check from landmark survival

If hazards are proportional, S1(t) = S0(t)HR at every t, so

HR = ln S1(t) / ln S0(t)

Invented example: 24-month event-free survival of 80% with treatment and 68% with control gives ln 0.80 / ln 0.68 = −0.223 / −0.386 ≈ 0.58. The reported HR uses the whole curve, so this is a rough check, not a reproduction. Kaplan–Meier percentages are estimates with their own uncertainty, and proportional hazards may not hold. A large gap between the check and the reported HR is a reason to look at the curves.

Questions for a reported hazard ratio

  • Which endpoint, and what counts as an event or as censoring?
  • Stratified or unstratified, adjusted for which covariates, and how were ties handled?
  • How many events in each arm, and how mature is follow-up?
  • Do the curves suggest proportional hazards, or a delayed or fading effect?
  • Is there a confidence interval, and which test produced the p-value?
  • Was this the prespecified primary analysis?
