Endpoints and estimands

An endpoint is the outcome being measured. An estimand is the precise treatment-effect question. A clear question comes before choosing the statistical test.

Numerical examples on this page are hypothetical unless a worked case study is explicitly linked.

Learn by changing the example

See three endpoints in the same patients

Move the cutoff date. Responses can accumulate while progression-free and overall survival decline.

Twelve hypothetical patients are fully observed through each cutoff, with no censoring. ORR counts anyone who has responded by then, even if they later progress. These are distinct measurements; changing how rescue treatment is handled would require a separate estimand.

An endpoint needs a definition and a clock

An endpoint specifies a measurement and how it is evaluated: response at a set visit, change in a symptom score by week 24, or time from randomization to an event. Primary endpoints address the main trial objective. Secondary endpoints add other planned information. Exploratory endpoints generate hypotheses.

Common oncology endpoints
EndpointWhat it measuresImportant distinction
Objective response rate (ORR)Proportion with a protocol-defined complete or partial responseTumor response does not by itself establish longer survival.
Duration of response (DOR)Time from response to progression or death, as defined in the protocolUsually conditions on having responded; it describes a selected subset.
Progression-free survival (PFS)Time to progression or deathScan schedule and progression assessment can influence results.
Overall survival (OS)Time to death from any causeFollow-up, crossover, and subsequent therapy affect interpretation.
Patient-reported outcome (PRO)Symptoms, functioning, or quality of life reported by patientsThe instrument, meaningful change, and missing reports matter.

Specify the treatment-effect question

Describe the population, treatment strategies, outcome, summary measure, and handling of intercurrent events: events after treatment starts that affect the outcome’s meaning or availability. Examples include stopping treatment, starting rescue medication, or death.

A treatment-policy question asks about outcomes under assignment to a strategy, including certain later treatment changes. A hypothetical question asks what might have happened under a specified alternative scenario, such as no rescue medication. These questions can need different data and assumptions. Death is not simply an ordinary missing symptom score.

Other strategies include a composite outcome that incorporates the event, a while-on-treatment question, or a principal-stratum question about a subgroup defined by potential events. They target different effects rather than interchangeable ways to improve a result.

One endpoint can answer different questions

Example: a symptom trial

A company measures symptom change at week 24. Some patients stop Drug A and receive rescue treatment. “What is the effect of assignment to A with rescue available?” differs from “What would A do if no one used rescue?” A treatment-policy analysis and a hypothetical analysis need not give the same answer.

The estimator is the procedure used to estimate the effect; the estimate is its numerical result. Neither should silently replace the question defined by the estimand.

An earlier measurement may be a surrogate

A surrogate endpoint is used to stand in for a clinical outcome of interest in a particular setting. An association between a biomarker and prognosis does not by itself show that changing the biomarker with treatment changes clinical benefit. The strength of the relationship can depend on disease, therapy, and context.

Read the exact endpoint wording, data cutoff, treatment-change rules, and clinical interpretation together. A headline saying “met the endpoint” is incomplete without them.
