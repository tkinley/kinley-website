Survival analysis, censoring, and hazard ratios

Time-to-event analysis uses both whether an event occurred and when it occurred. It also handles patients whose event time is not fully observed, through explicit censoring assumptions.

Numerical examples on this page are hypothetical unless a worked case study is explicitly linked.

What censoring says

For OS, a patient alive at the last known follow-up is right-censored: the event time is later than the observed follow-up time. Censoring is not an event and does not mean that the patient will never experience one. For other endpoints, such as PFS, the protocol defines both events and censoring rules.

Common methods require censoring to be noninformative under the relevant model or conditional assumptions. Losing patients because they are deteriorating can violate that assumption. Administrative censoring at a common data cutoff is different from unexplained loss to follow-up.

Kaplan–Meier updates survival at observed event times

At each event time, multiply the previous survival estimate by (1 − events / number at risk immediately before that time). Censoring reduces the later risk set without causing a downward step at the censoring time.

A hypothetical five-patient example
TimeWhat happensEstimated survival
Month 21 event among 5 at risk1 × (4/5) = 0.80
Month 31 patient censoredRemains 0.80; later risk set becomes 3
Month 41 event among 3 at risk0.80 × (2/3) ≈ 0.533

Read the numbers at risk and confidence intervals under the curve. A dramatic late separation based on very few remaining patients is less precise than the line alone may suggest.

Median survival and landmark survival

The KM median is the first time the estimated survival curve reaches 50% or below. “Median not reached” means the estimate has not crossed that level by the analyzed follow-up; it does not establish an arbitrarily long survival benefit.

A landmark survival estimate, such as S(12 months), summarizes the curve at a prespecified time. It needs an uncertainty interval and adequate follow-up. Comparing medians alone ignores other parts of the distribution.

A hazard ratio is not a risk ratio

A hazard is an instantaneous event rate among those still event-free. Under a proportional-hazards model, HR = 0.70 represents a treatment hazard 30% lower relative to control over time. It does not mean 30% longer survival or that 30% fewer patients have an event at every time point.

If hazards cross or treatment effects change with time, a constant HR can be a poor summary. A Cox model estimate depends on the data and model; examine curves and proportional-hazards assumptions. A log-rank test assesses differences between survival distributions and has greatest efficiency under proportional-hazards alternatives.

Restricted mean survival time offers a time-scale summary

RMST is the area under the survival curve up to a chosen horizon τ. An RMST difference of two months through 24 months means two additional average event-free or survival months within that window, depending on the endpoint. It is not an unrestricted lifetime gain.

The endpoint, horizon, censoring rules, follow-up maturity, effect measure, and prespecified analysis should be read together.
