Response rates and binomial probability

A response rate is a count divided by a denominator. The arithmetic is simple; the clinical definition, analysis population, and uncertainty determine what that number means.

Numerical examples on this page are hypothetical unless a worked case study is explicitly linked.

Observed rate versus true probability

If x of n patients meet a response definition, the observed proportion is p̂ = x/n. The unknown response probability in a defined population is p. A sample proportion estimates p under a model; it does not reveal it exactly.

Example: 18 responses

Drug A produces 18 responses among 40 treated patients: 18/40 = 45%. If a report excludes ten patients without an evaluable scan and reports 18/30 = 60%, the higher rate comes from a different denominator. The exclusion might be prespecified, but its consequences still need explanation.

Confirmed and unconfirmed responses also differ. Confirmation rules, minimum response duration, assessor, and assessment schedule should be stated.

What a binomial model assumes

For n independent patients with the same response probability p, the responder count X follows a binomial distribution. Its expected value is np and its variance is np(1−p).

P(X = k) = C(n, k) pk(1−p)n−k

C(n, k) counts the ways k responses can occur among n patients. The model allows random variation even when p stays fixed. It does not handle an unknown p merely by inserting a point estimate and calling it certain.

A count probability is a conditional calculation

Example: ten future patients

Assume p = 0.30 for ten independent patients. The expected count is 3, but P(X ≥ 4) is approximately 35.0%. An expected value is an average across repetitions, not a guarantee of three responses.

Changing p changes the distribution. Uncertainty about p can be modeled explicitly with a Bayesian predictive model; differences in future eligibility or care need additional assumptions. Neither a binomial tail probability nor an ORR is a probability of regulatory approval.

Questions before comparing two rates

  • Are the response definitions, confirmation rules, and follow-up comparable?
  • Are all randomized patients, all treated patients, or only evaluable patients included?
  • Were missing outcomes excluded or handled through a prespecified rule?
  • Was the population chosen before looking at outcomes?
  • What is the confidence interval, and is the comparison randomized?

A response rate in one arm describes that arm. Establishing an incremental treatment effect requires a defensible comparison.
