Trial design, controls, and randomization

A drug result only makes sense in the design that produced it. Before reading a response rate or p-value, identify who entered the trial, what they received, and what the investigators compared.

Numerical examples on this page are hypothetical unless a worked case study is explicitly linked.

What the design is trying to do

A single-arm trial gives everyone the study treatment. It can describe outcomes and identify activity, but an apparent improvement over historical experience may reflect differences in patients, care, or assessment. A randomized controlled trial assigns patients by chance to treatment strategies, making a causal comparison more credible when the trial is conducted and analyzed appropriately.

Randomization balances measured and unmeasured baseline characteristics in expectation, not perfectly in every sample. Allocation concealment prevents the upcoming assignment from influencing enrollment. Blinding reduces the influence of knowing treatment on care, reporting, or outcome assessment; it is different from concealment.

Choose the comparison that answers the question

A control can be placebo, active treatment, standard care, or standard care plus placebo. Placebo does not necessarily mean withholding effective therapy: both arms may receive a common standard treatment. An external control uses patients outside the randomized trial and needs a strong explanation of comparability.

Example: an add-on treatment

Compare standard care plus Drug A with the same standard care plus placebo. The contrast estimates the incremental effect of adding A under that treatment strategy. Comparing A in heavily pretreated patients with an older first-line study would answer a much less reliable question.

Trial phases describe purpose

Phase 1 usually emphasizes dose, tolerability, and pharmacology; Phase 2 explores activity and refines the treatment strategy; Phase 3 usually tests confirmatory benefit and risk. Designs can overlap these purposes. A phase label alone does not establish the reliability of an endpoint, and a large uncontrolled study does not acquire randomization through its size.

Eligibility restrictions also affect generalizability. Results in a selected, healthier population may not transfer unchanged to patients with different disease severity, prior therapy, or organ function.

What to look for in a company readout

  • Was assignment randomized, and was the ratio 1:1 or something else?
  • Did both arms receive comparable background care and follow-up?
  • Who was blinded, and were subjective endpoints independently assessed?
  • Were important baseline imbalances present?
  • Does the analysis preserve the planned comparison?

Randomization supports the design; missing outcomes, treatment crossover, and selective reporting still need to be examined.
