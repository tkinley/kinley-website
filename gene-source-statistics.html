Difference = pA − pB
Prevalence ratio = pA / pB
Odds ratio = [pA / (1 − pA)] / [pB / (1 − pB)]

Multiply a difference in proportions by 100 to get percentage points. A difference of 0.25 is 25 pp.

Structure

Related observations

Bacterial lineages share ancestry and often gene content. Pooling them can hide meaningful group differences.

Confounding

An uneven mix

If lineage relates to both source and gene carriage, the pooled association may reflect lineage composition.

Adjustment

Compare within groups

Stratify by lineage or include suitable covariates in a model. Check that both sources are represented.

Noncollapsibility: pooled and conditional odds ratios can differ even without confounding. A changed adjusted OR alone does not prove bias.

The mix problem also appears with age groups, hospitals, and study sites. A pooled result can disappear or even reverse after stratifying.

Separate mix from within-lineage differences

Pooled prevalence in A = Σ (A’s lineage share × A’s prevalence in that lineage)
Within-lineage difference Δk = pA,k − pB,k

Σ means “add across lineages”; k labels a lineage. Group B has its own lineage shares in the pooled comparison.

Here, ΔX = 1 − 1 = 0 and ΔY = 0 − 0 = 0. Unequal shares still produce a pooled gap.

Why answers differ

Effect heterogeneity

When lineage-specific effects differ, changing the reference mix changes the summary.

Choose weights to match the question. Group A, the full cohort, and equal-lineage weighting describe different targets.

Document weights before interpreting the result. Standardization describes an association; a causal interpretation needs additional assumptions.

Weight, then add

Δstandardized = Σ wk × (pA,k − pB,k)
All wk ≥ 0; Σ wk = 1

Use the same weights for A and B. This averages prevalence differences, not odds ratios.

What does the evidence support?

Effect size, uncertainty, and assumptions

Magnitude

Effect size

How large is the observed difference? Start with counts and a clearly named measure.

Example: +25 pp
Precision

Confidence interval

An interval procedure expressing sampling uncertainty under stated assumptions. Wider intervals mean less precision.

Compatibility

P-value

Under the null hypothesis and test assumptions, the probability of a statistic at least as extreme as observed.

A small p-value does not measure effect size, establish causation, or give the probability that the null is true.

What does “95% confidence” mean?

Across repeated samples under the assumptions, a valid 95% interval procedure covers the fixed population quantity about 95% of the time. It is not a claim that 95% of observations fall inside the interval.

Selected observations support a descriptive comparison. Population inference additionally needs defensible sampling or model assumptions. ASA: p-values · NIST: confidence intervals

Build a comparison under the null

  1. Define the test. Choose the null, statistic, and what counts as “extreme.”
  2. Shuffle within lineages. Rearrange source labels within each lineage, keeping source totals fixed there.
  3. Recalculate. Repeat the same analysis, including any prespecified weighting rule.
  4. Compare. Count how often the shuffled statistic is at least as extreme as the observed statistic.
Monte Carlo p = (1 + extreme shuffled results) / (1 + number of shuffles)

Exchangeability: the permitted label swaps must be valid under the null. Lineage stratification alone does not guarantee this.

Additional dependence may require restricted swaps or a different model. A permutation p-value is not a confidence interval and does not repair selection bias.

Dependence
Shared ancestry or sampling history reduces independent information. Ten clusters do not establish ten independent sampling events.
Selection
A selected genome collection may not represent the wider population. More genomes alone do not remove selection bias.
Sparse counts
Few observations or zero cells can make ratios unstable. Choose an inference method suitable for the design and counts.
Multiple testing
Testing many genes creates more opportunities for false positives. Define the test family and use a suitable multiplicity procedure.
Biological claims
Gene–source association alone does not establish adaptation, persistence, desiccation tolerance, or transmission.

Report the effect, uncertainty, population, and assumptions together. A threshold alone cannot do that work.

Check your understanding

Recall, definitions, and further study

Answer first, then open the explanation.

1. Does an odds ratio of 3 mean three times the probability?

No. It means three times the odds. At 75% vs 50%, the odds ratio is 3 and the prevalence ratio is 1.5.

2. A pooled gap disappears within every lineage. Why?

The lineage mix may explain it. One source group may contain more of the lineages where the gene is common.

3. Why can two valid weighting schemes give different answers?

They target different populations. When within-lineage differences vary, the reference weights affect the summary.

4. Does a small p-value prove a large or causal effect?

No. Read the effect size and interval, examine assumptions, and distinguish association from causation.

5. Are 64 genomes necessarily 64 independent observations?

No. Shared ancestry, repeated sampling, or common outbreaks can create dependence. Clustering does not automatically eliminate it.

6. Does a change in the adjusted odds ratio prove confounding?

No. Odds ratios are noncollapsible: a conditional and a pooled OR can differ even without confounding.

Where the gene–source example began

All interactive gene counts and two-lineage examples here are invented for teaching. They do not reproduce published gene-level results. Reference weights are teaching choices, not a prescribed analysis of that cohort.
