01 / Compare fairly

The lineage trap

Imagine two genetic lineages. Every genome in lineage A carries the gene. No genome in lineage B carries it. We now sample different numbers of LMF and other-source genomes from each lineage. Does the gene suddenly become a food-associated gene?

See the equations in plain English
Pooled difference = (gene-positive LMF / all LMF) − (gene-positive Other / all Other)

Within-lineage difference: do the same calculation separately inside A and B. Here it is always 100% − 100% = 0 in A and 0% − 0% = 0 in B. Both sources are represented in both lineages at every slider setting.

Term: Population structure. Bacteria are not all unrelated; genetic lineages can differ in gene content. If our source groups contain different proportions of those lineages, an overall association may reflect lineage composition rather than a difference within lineages.

02 / Measure the difference

Probability is not odds

Here's the shortcut: probability = YES / ALL, while odds = YES / NO. To compare two groups using an odds ratio, divide their odds.

Compares odds, not probabilities.

The gene is 25 percentage points more prevalent in LMF. The LMF odds are 3 times the Other odds. That does not mean the gene is 3 times as probable.

See the equations in plain English
Probability = carriers / total
Odds = carriers / noncarriers
Odds ratio = (LMF odds) / (Other odds)

At 18/24 and 20/40: probability is 75% versus 50%; odds are 18/6 = 3 versus 20/20 = 1; odds ratio is 3. With zero cells, ordinary odds or odds ratios may be zero, infinite, or undefined. The page shows these cases rather than inserting made-up corrections.

Term: Covariate. An additional variable included in a model. In our lineage-aware comparison, genetic lineage is an adjustment variable; isolation source is the group comparison and gene presence is what we count. The roles of source and gene can reverse in a different model specification.

Term: Noncollapsibility. Even if lineage is not confounding the association, a pooled odds ratio can differ from an odds ratio conditioned on lineage. A change after adjustment alone does not prove confounding.

03 / Define the comparison

Which lineages count more?

To compare sources fairly, we can first compare them within each lineage and then combine those differences. But we have to decide how much weight each lineage gets. Different weights answer different questions.

For the real 64-genome cohort, there are ten terms rather than two. In the A1-D1 discussion, LMF-based weighting was favored as the reference, while all-cohort weighting remained an alternative. These choices define different comparisons. They are not universal rules.

Term: Estimand. The exact quantity a study tries to measure. The weights are part of the estimand: choosing a different reference lineage mixture changes what your comparison means.

Term: Standardization. Combining within-group comparisons using the same stated lineage weights, rather than letting the two source groups have different lineage mixtures.

04 / Interpret carefully

What the numbers cannot prove

Our sample is useful for describing selected genomes. But a difference calculated from 64 genomes is not automatically a fact about every Salmonella Enteritidis population.

Descriptive result

“In these selected genomes, gene prevalence differs by this many percentage points.” We can calculate that directly if the gene calls and metadata are correct.

Permutation test

Ask how unusual a statistic would be if source labels were rearranged within lineages under a specific null hypothesis. This requires an exchangeability assumption; it is not a confidence interval.

Confidence interval

An interval for an unknown population quantity depends on assumptions about sampling, independence, or a model. It does not appear automatically just because we have 64 genomes.

Phylogenetic dependence

Two similar genomes may share ancestry or sampling history. Ten SNP clusters help us compare within lineages, but they do not guarantee 64 independent sampling events or remove all remaining genetic structure.

A four-question check before making a claim

  1. What exactly did I compare? Gene prevalence, odds, or another quantity? Among which genomes?
  2. Did I examine lineage? Could the pooled association come from unequal lineage representation?
  3. What uncertainty is justified? Did I specify the sampling assumptions or null hypothesis?
  4. Am I overclaiming biology? Gene–source association alone does not prove adaptation, persistence, desiccation tolerance, or transmission.

Quick self-check

1. An OR of 3 means a gene is three times as probable in LMF. True or false?

False. The odds are three times as high. Probability and odds are different measures.

2. A pooled gene association disappears within every lineage. What might explain it?

Unequal lineage composition between source groups. This is why the pooled comparison alone cannot distinguish lineage-driven patterns from within-lineage differences.

3. Does having 10 SNP clusters mean we have 10 independent sampling events?

No. Genetic clustering and independent biological sampling events are separate concepts.

4. Does a significant gene–source association establish an adaptation to low-moisture food?

No. It describes a statistical pattern. An adaptive mechanism requires additional biological evidence.

Why this matters for AI agents

An agent should not simply report the smallest p-value. It should recognize the comparison being made, account for population structure where appropriate, explain its assumptions, and avoid stronger biological claims than the evidence supports. More than one statistical method can be defensible.
