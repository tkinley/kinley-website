Concept 4.1

Calculate a standard error

Assume 100 independent, comparable binary observations and 40 successes.

  1. Estimate p with 40/100 = 0.40.
  2. The plug-in variance of the proportion is 0.40 × 0.60 ÷ 100 = 0.0024.
  3. Take its square root: SE ≈ 0.049, or 4.9 percentage points.

The SE measures sampling variability under the model. A valid 95% confidence interval procedure covers the fixed target about 95% of the time across repetitions under its assumptions.

Try it · What happens if the sample size quadruples and the rate stays fixed?

The SE is halved because it scales with 1/√n under independence.

Concept 4.2

Why the Wald interval collapses

A normal-approximation interval takes the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors.

  1. At 0/10, the observed proportion is zero.
  2. The plug-in SE is √(0 × 1 ÷ 10) = 0.
  3. The Wald formula yields [0, 0]. Yet if the true p were 0.10, the chance of seeing zero successes would be 0.9¹⁰ ≈ 0.35.

The zero-width interval is a failure of the plug-in approximation at the boundary.

Try it · What happens in the same formula at 10/10?

The plug-in SE is again zero, and the formula collapses to [1, 1].

Concept 4.3

Work out a Wilson interval

Wilson inverts a score test whose standard error depends on the hypothesized p. At an observed zero, the simplified upper limit is easy to calculate.

  1. Use z = 1.96, so z² ≈ 3.8416.
  2. For x = 0 and n = 10, the upper limit is z² ÷ (n + z²).
  3. Compute 3.8416 ÷ 13.8416 ≈ 0.2775. The lower limit is zero.

The Wilson 95% interval is approximately [0%, 27.8%].

Try it · What is the Wilson upper limit for 0/20?

3.8416 ÷ 23.8416 ≈ 16.1%.

Concept 4.4

Update a Jeffreys prior

A Bayesian interval starts with a prior for p and updates it with the binomial likelihood.

  1. Use the Jeffreys prior Beta(½, ½) for one binomial rate.
  2. After x successes in n trials, update to Beta(x + ½, n − x + ½).
  3. At 0/10 the posterior is Beta(0.5, 10.5). Take its 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles for an equal-tailed 95% credible interval.

The interval describes posterior uncertainty conditional on the model, prior, and data.

Try it · What is the posterior at 6/10?

Beta(6.5, 4.5).

Concept 4.5

Combine uncertainty for a difference

A confidence interval for A − B needs uncertainty from both group rates. Newcombe’s Wilson method combines asymmetric offsets for independent proportions.

  1. Suppose A = 6/10 and B = 3/10, giving an estimate of 0.30. Their Wilson intervals are about [0.3127, 0.8318] and [0.1078, 0.6032].
  2. For the lower offset use A’s distance down and B’s distance up: √[(0.60 − 0.3127)² + (0.6032 − 0.30)²] ≈ 0.4177.
  3. For the upper offset use A’s distance up and B’s distance down: √[(0.8318 − 0.60)² + (0.30 − 0.1078)²] ≈ 0.3011. Apply the offsets to 0.30.

The resulting interval is about [−11.8, +60.1] pp. The sample estimates a +30 pp gap with substantial uncertainty.

Try it · Why does the lower bound use B’s upper offset?

Increasing the subtracted B rate decreases A − B.

Concept 4.6

Propagate variance through weights

For fixed weights and independent stratum-level estimates, use squared weights when combining variances.

  1. Let the stratum SEs be 0.10 and 0.20 and the weights be 0.25 and 0.75.
  2. Add weighted variances: 0.25² × 0.10² + 0.75² × 0.20² = 0.023125.
  3. Take the square root: SE ≈ 0.152. If the estimates are dependent, the variance also includes covariance terms.

The 75%-weighted stratum contributes most of the variability in this example.

Try it · Why square the weights?

Multiplying a random variable by w multiplies its variance by w².
