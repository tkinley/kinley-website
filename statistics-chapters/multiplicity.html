Concept 6.1

Count informative strata

For a fixed-margin test, a stratum contributes label-swap information only when both groups are present and the outcome varies there.

  1. Batch X has 4/4 successes and both groups present. Every swap preserves the same rates.
  2. Batch Y has 2/4 successes and both groups present. Swapping can change who gets those successes.
  3. There is one informative stratum. Report the count rather than relying on the total sample size.

A constant within-stratum outcome can produce an observed difference of zero while leaving population uncertainty.

Try it · What if all strata are constant?

The null statistic is fixed under every permitted swap. An exact tail count with ties included yields p = 1.

Concept 6.2

Build a label-blind information inventory

Assess what can be learned from group sizes and stratum outcome totals before looking at group-specific outcomes.

  1. Record nA, nB, and total successes m separately in each stratum.
  2. The smallest possible A success count is max(0, m − nB). The largest is min(m, nA).
  3. With nA = 3, nB = 2, and m = 4, A can have either 2 or 3 successes.

The margins reveal how much the statistic can vary without revealing the observed group allocation.

Try it · What if m = nA + nB?

Both limits equal nA, so the A count cannot vary.

Concept 6.3

Find the smallest attainable p-value

A discrete test has finitely many tables, so even its most extreme table may fail to reach a threshold.

  1. Calculate the test statistic and p-value for each legal outcome under the fixed margins.
  2. Take the smallest p-value across those outcomes for the chosen tail rule.
  3. In Chapter 5’s two-stratum example, both extremes give 2/36 = 0.0556 under the two-sided rule.

At a 0.05 cutoff, this two-sided test cannot reject for those margins.

Try it · Is 0.0556 the smallest p for every statistic or tail rule?

No. It applies to this statistic, these margins, and the specified two-sided rule.

Concept 6.4

Control familywise error

Define the family of tests before selecting which results to report.

  1. For 4 tests and α = 0.05, use the Bonferroni cutoff 0.05/4 = 0.0125.
  2. Compare each valid p-value to the common cutoff.
  3. In Figure 6, only 0.004 is below 0.0125.

Bonferroni controls the chance of at least one false rejection across the family. Discrete-data refinements use the attainable bounds under a specified procedure.

Try it · Does an arbitrary filter after seeing the group differences preserve the guarantee?

No general guarantee applies. Use a prespecified procedure whose error control covers the filtering rule.

Concept 6.5

Apply the Benjamini–Hochberg rule

FDR is the expected proportion of false rejections among rejections, defined as zero on runs with no rejections.

  1. Sort the four p-values from smallest to largest.
  2. Compare p at rank i to i × 0.05/4.
  3. The largest passing rank is 2 because 0.018 ≤ 0.025. Reject ranks 1 and 2.

The 5% target concerns the expected proportion under the procedure’s assumptions. It does not certify a particular discovery.

Try it · Why look for the largest passing rank?

BH is a step-up rule. Every p-value at or below that rank is rejected.

Concept 6.6

Report a screen with effect sizes

A screening result can be precise, unstable, or limited by test resolution. Put those distinctions alongside the adjusted evidence.

  1. Report the test family, valid nulls, and multiplicity procedure.
  2. Show an effect estimate, interval, and supporting counts for results of interest.
  3. State which features have little or no within-stratum information and which could not reach the testing threshold.

A table of adjusted p-values is a starting point for interpretation.

Try it · What would you show when an estimated difference is large and the interval is wide?

Show the point estimate, interval, and sparse supporting counts. Describe the uncertainty in the size and direction where relevant.
