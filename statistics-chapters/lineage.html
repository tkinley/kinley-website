Compare within strata

Separate a difference within groups from a difference in group composition.

Concept 2.1

Choose the strata

A stratum is a subgroup defined before computing the comparison: a batch, site, or other relevant grouping variable.

  1. Identify the outcome (yes/no) and the exposure or group (A/B).
  2. Decide which variable defines comparable units, here batches X and Y.
  3. Build a separate 2×2 table for each batch. Record sample sizes alongside rates.

Within-stratum and pooled comparisons use different denominators.

Try it · What are the totals for A inside X and inside Y in Figure 2?

8 in X and 2 in Y.

Concept 2.2

Calculate the pooled contrast

Pooling combines outcomes and totals across batches.

  1. Sum A across batches: (8 + 0) ÷ (8 + 2) = 0.80.
  2. Sum B: (2 + 0) ÷ (2 + 8) = 0.20.
  3. Subtract: 0.80 − 0.20 = +0.60, or +60 pp.

The pooled gap is a comparison of the observed mixtures.

Try it · Why would simply averaging the batch rates give 50% for both?

An equal average puts 50% weight on both batches. The pooled calculation uses each group’s observed weights.

Concept 2.3

Calculate within-stratum contrasts

Hold batch fixed and compare A and B inside the same batch.

  1. Inside X: 8/8 − 2/2 = 1 − 1 = 0.
  2. Inside Y: 0/2 − 0/8 = 0 − 0 = 0.
  3. Compare these two zeros with the +60 pp pooled gap.

The pooled gap comes entirely from different batch composition in this example.

Try it · What would happen if both groups had half their observations from X?

Their pooled rates would both be 50%. The within-batch gaps would still be zero.

Concept 2.4

Decompose a pooled rate

A pooled rate is a weighted average, with each group using its own batch proportions.

  1. A’s batch shares are 8/10 and 2/10. Compute 0.8 × 1 + 0.2 × 0 = 0.8.
  2. B’s shares are 2/10 and 8/10. Compute 0.2 × 1 + 0.8 × 0 = 0.2.
  3. The within-batch rates are identical. Only the weights differ.

Check both the subgroup rates and the subgroup shares when diagnosing a pooled result.

Try it · Can pooling reverse the direction of an association?

Yes. Unequal subgroup weights can outweigh the within-group differences.

Concept 2.5

Diagnose lack of overlap

If batch Z contains 12 units in A and none in B, B’s rate in Z is unobserved.

  1. Write A’s rate using its 12 observations.
  2. Identify B’s rate as undefined: its denominator is zero.
  3. Choose a target supported by both groups or explicitly state any extrapolation assumptions.

A group with zero observations is different from a group with observations and zero successes.

Try it · Can a directly standardized estimate assign positive weight to Z without extra assumptions?

No. The B rate in Z is missing, so Z’s within-batch difference cannot be calculated directly.

Concept 2.6

Distinguish confounding from noncollapsibility

Even with identical stratum shares, a pooled odds ratio can differ from a common within-stratum odds ratio.

  1. Let B’s rates be 10% in X and 50% in Y. Set the within-stratum odds ratio to 2.
  2. Doubling B’s odds gives A’s rates of 2/11 ≈ 18.2% in X and 2/3 ≈ 66.7% in Y.
  3. With equal shares in both groups, the pooled rates are about 42.4% and 30%. Their odds ratio is about 1.72.

A change from 2.00 to 1.72 can come from averaging on a different scale. Assess confounding using the design and the role of each covariate.

Try it · What would equal shares do for a prevalence difference?

The pooled difference would equal the average of the within-stratum differences with those shared weights.
