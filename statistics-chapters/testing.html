Null hypotheses and p-values

A null hypothesis specifies a reference explanation, such as a zero mean difference. A test statistic measures departure from that reference. The p-value is the probability, under the null and test assumptions, of a statistic at least as extreme as observed using the specified tail rule.

Intuition

A reference distribution tells you how far noise can push the statistic when the null model holds. An unusual statistic is evidence about compatibility with that reference.

Worked math

  1. For an estimate of 0.10 with a null-compatible SE of 0.05, z = (0.10 − 0)/0.05 = 2.
  2. Under a standard-normal null, the two-sided tail probability is P(|Z| ≥ 2) ≈ 0.0455.
  3. A p-value depends on precision as well as effect size. The same 0.10 effect with SE 0.10 gives z = 1 and p ≈ 0.317.

In bioinformatics

When reading a feature table, look at estimated effect, precision, and design together. A p-value alone does not identify a large effect.

Permutation and exchangeability

A permutation test replaces a theoretical null with a reference distribution built from permissible label assignments. It requires exchangeability for the chosen swaps under the test’s null. Blocks, pairs, and repeated measurements limit which swaps the design supports.

Intuition

A shuffled assignment should look like something the original study could plausibly have produced under the null. Swapping between unrelated batches can change the design itself.

Worked math

  1. In one block with two success and two failure labels, place two records in group A. There are 6 equally likely assignments.
  2. The A success counts 0, 1, and 2 occur in 1, 4, and 1 assignments respectively.
  3. A two-sided absolute-deviation test centered at 1 gives p = (1 + 1)/6 = 0.333 if A gets both successes. Discrete tests can have coarse resolution.

In bioinformatics

For paired comparisons, preserve pairs. For blocked comparisons, justify swaps within blocks before interpreting a tail probability.

Type I error, Type II error, and power

Type I error is rejecting a true null. Type II error is failing to reject under a specified alternative. Power is one minus the Type II error probability, for that alternative and design. Sample size, noise, effect size, and thresholds affect power.

Intuition

Two overlapping sampling distributions describe results under the null and under a particular alternative. The rejection threshold controls how much of each distribution falls into the rejection region.

Worked math

  1. Suppose a prespecified directional test rejects when Z > 1.645, giving α ≈ 0.05 under Z ~ Normal(0, 1).
  2. If a meaningful effect shifts the mean of Z to 2 while keeping its SD at 1, power is P(Z > 1.645 | mean 2).
  3. Standardize: P(Normal(0,1) > −0.355) ≈ 0.64.

In bioinformatics

A larger sample can improve power by decreasing the SE. Increasing the number of technical records per specimen has a different effect from recruiting independent specimens.

Familywise error and false discovery rate

Familywise error rate is the probability of at least one false rejection in a defined test family. False discovery rate (FDR) is the expected fraction of false rejections among rejections, with that fraction defined as zero if nothing is rejected. The two criteria support different decisions.

Intuition

A short list for expensive confirmatory work may need tight familywise control. An exploratory screen often accepts some false discoveries to find more signals, then evaluates follow-up evidence.

Worked math

  2. For BH at 5% FDR, sort the p-values and find the largest passing rank using i × 0.05/4.

In bioinformatics

Define the family before selecting results. Include effect sizes and supporting counts when presenting discoveries.

Practical significance and equivalence

A comparison can be statistically detectable and too small to matter. Conversely, a wide interval can include both no effect and a meaningful effect. Equivalence testing requires a margin defined by the application and evidence precise enough to support it.

Intuition

An assay team can decide that a shift of less than 5 percentage points is operationally negligible. That defines a target region for the interval.

Worked math

  1. With margin ±5 pp, an interval [−2, 3] lies within the negligible region.
  2. An interval [−9, 5] crosses beyond the lower margin. Its inclusion of zero still leaves meaningful decreases compatible with the data.
  3. For two one-sided tests at α = 0.05, the matching two-sided confidence level is generally 90% when the tests and interval use the same construction.

In bioinformatics

When comparing platforms, state the smallest difference that changes the scientific decision before judging the estimate.

Practice questions

1. Separate the error criteria

Apply Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg at 5% to p-values 0.004, 0.018, 0.041, 0.200. Which tests are rejected?

Show the answer

Bonferroni rejects rank 1. BH rejects ranks 1 and 2 because 2 is the largest passing rank.

2. Explain an inconclusive result

A difference is +8 pp with an interval from −3 to +19 pp. Interpret it and explain whether it supports equivalence within ±5 pp.

Show the answer

The observed estimate favors A by 8 pp. The interval covers zero and positive changes that could matter. It extends outside ±5 pp, so it does not support equivalence within that margin.
