Why I am still cautious

I do not think the fall in IOVA’s share price is enough to say it is cheap. I want to know how much the business could earn and how much of that future I am already paying for.

The part I give the most value to is the approved melanoma business: Amtagvi and Proleukin. Together, they contribute $4.98 per share before I deduct $0.35 for risk to the existing label. Lung cancer adds only $0.11 in my model.

That is why I keep coming back to melanoma. How many patients can Iovance treat? What margin can it make? How much demand could competing treatments take? And what happens if TILVANCE-301 disappoints? Those questions move my valuation much more than a small change in the lung assumptions.

At the $8.48 price I used in my notes, I think I would already be paying for a better outcome than my base assumptions support. I can get to that price, but I need stronger sales, higher margins, better pipeline results, or a lower required return. I want to be clear about which of those I am assuming before calling the shares undervalued.

This is my opinion as of 30 October 2026, not investment advice. All amounts are U.S. dollars. I compare against $8.48, IOVA’s closing price on 30 October 2026.

Review supplement · 1 November 2026

Start with the disclosed safety constraints.

The FDA prescribing information reports 12 treatment-related deaths among 160 patients who initiated the Amtagvi regimen (7.5%): two during lymphodepletion, six within 30 days of administration and four later deaths. This is a regimen-level trial figure, including lymphodepletion and IL-2, not proof of undisclosed events in a different study. Trial rates may differ from practice.

The label requires inpatient administration with an intensive-care facility and appropriately experienced specialists available. That affects eligibility, referral and delivery economics. I now make those constraints explicit in the patient bridge instead of presenting ordinary known safety risks only as a hypothetical surprise. I have not silently added a second haircut to the archived valuation.

FDA prescribing information, sections 2.1, 5.1 and 6.1. The retrospective lung release and its absence of a full AE table do not establish concealment or misconduct.

How I work out the value

I start with the approved melanoma business. I estimate sales and margins under three different outcomes, work out what the future profits are worth today, and give each outcome a weight. Then I add the other programs, allowing for the chance that they fail.

This is a sum-of-the-parts risk-adjusted net present value, or rNPV. The reason I use it is that an approved product and an experimental program should not get the same treatment in the calculation.

Equity = core DCF − expected label loss + pipeline rNPVs + next-generation option − corporate costs + net cash
Value per share = equity ÷ diluted shares

For each experimental program, I take the value of profits if it succeeds, multiply by my assumed chance of success, and subtract development spending. I charge the scheduled development costs in full here. That is a simplification: I have not modeled every possible stopping point along the way.

I use a 10% discount rate as my required return. I already account for clinical and regulatory failure through the success probabilities, so I do not add another large discount-rate penalty for the same risk.

301 is especially important to me because it affects two parts of the value. Success could add frontline melanoma sales. Failure could also put the existing accelerated approval at risk. I use a 50% chance of success, a 20% chance of withdrawal if it fails, and a 70% loss of core value if withdrawal happens. That gives an expected deduction of 50% × 20% × 70% = 7% of core value. These are my assumptions; they are not probabilities supplied by the FDA.

I value cash flows from 2027 through 2045, discounted to roughly the end of 2026. I allow $40M a year for corporate costs and 21% cash tax on positive profits from 2033. I add no takeover premium and no value for cash flows after 2045.

Clinical evidence and a modeling limitation

Frontline response and confirmation are different questions.

Iovance’s 23 October 2026 ESMO announcement reported a 62.5% confirmed response rate and 29.2% complete-response rate in the final Phase 2 frontline analysis. For 15/24 responses, the ordinary 95% Wilson interval is approximately 42.7–78.8%. That remains single-arm evidence in a selected population, not a randomized comparison or an estimate of Phase 3 success probability.

TILVANCE-301 has dual primary endpoints: an interim ORR analysis and a final PFS analysis, with OS as a key secondary endpoint. The trial is intended to support a frontline filing and confirmation of the existing post-PD-1 indication. An ORR win and a failure to verify confirmatory benefit are not logically the same event. I use 20% for sarcoma and 15% for endometrial cancer because I want each program to show its own evidence. I do not assume that success in melanoma automatically carries over to another cancer. The exact percentages are judgment calls; the response data do not produce them for me.

For each pipeline program, I assume a five-year ramp to peak sales, a plateau through 2038, and an 8% annual decline after that. Margins start at 15% and reach the mature level three years after launch. Those dates and ramps are assumptions I use to calculate value.

I give the next-generation programs a combined $100M of value. I see them as possible upside, but I am not ready to build a large sales forecast around them. This is one of the more subjective parts of the model.

Finally, I use $240M net cash and 475M diluted shares. Both need updating as new reports come out. If Iovance raises more money, I need to consider the cash received and the additional shares together.

Why I moved from $7.13 to $5.83

Revision context, clarified 1 November 2026: TUDRIQEV was approved on 6 August 2026, before both versions. The competition changes are a reassessment of a known risk, not a newly announced October approval. The published model records a $0.49/share lung reduction and about $0.54/share from core weights and erosion; it does not attribute the entire share-price decline to one program. Both versions and their assumptions remain available. Sponsor’s approval disclosure.

I lowered several assumptions from my first pass. Here is what I changed, why I changed it, and how much each change took off the valuation.

  1. $7.13 · Where I started

    My earlier assumptions gave me $7.13 per share.

  2. $6.64 · I lowered my lung assumptions

    The broader response rate and the selected subgroup leave me wanting more evidence on durability and prospective results. I lowered success from 45% to 30%, cut peak sales from $500M to $350M, moved launch to 2030, and allowed more development spending.

    −$0.49 per share from this change.
  3. $6.20 · I put more weight on weaker melanoma sales

    With another approved treatment competing for patients, I am less comfortable giving the strongest sales path a large weight. My bear/base/bull weights move from 30/50/20 to 40/45/15.

    −$0.44 per share from this change.
  4. $6.10 · I allowed sales to fade faster after their peak

    I expect competition to matter over time. I added two percentage points to the annual decline after peak sales in each core scenario. The size of that effect is my judgment.

    −$0.10 per share from this change.
  5. $6.09 · I lowered my 2027 base revenue estimate

    I moved from $600M to $575M. That still assumes substantial growth from the midpoint of 2026 guidance.

    −$0.01 per share from this change.
  6. $6.01 · I allowed more risk to the existing label

    If 301 fails, I use a 20% conditional chance of withdrawal rather than 15%. I think it is worth allowing for a less favorable regulatory outcome, although the FDA has not assigned either probability.

    −$0.09 per share from this change.
  7. $5.83 · I gave the other programs less value

    I lowered sarcoma success to 20%, endometrial success to 15%, and the next-generation option to $100M. I want each program to earn more value with its own evidence.

    −$0.17 per share from this change.

Most of the reduction came from giving lung less value and being more cautious about melanoma sales. I have kept the changes separate so it is possible to disagree with one of them without rejecting the whole calculation.

Why $8.48 is harder for me to justify

The earlier comparison price, with the rest of v2 fixed

The same single-path reverse DCF requires about $2.98B in peak annual core revenue at $14.81, compared with $1.50B at $8.48. Holding every other v2 input fixed is a sensitivity exercise; it does not identify the market’s actual lung expectations or explain the cause of the price move. The $1B single-path case is $6.35/share, while the weighted central case remains $5.83.

I also work backward from the price. I keep the pipeline and base margins the same, then ask how large the melanoma business would need to become for me to get $8.48 per share.

About $1.50B in annual core revenueThat is the peak sales level I need in this calculation to reach $8.48 per share.

For this test, sales start at $575M in 2027, rise steadily to the peak in 2031, stay there through 2035, and decline 10% a year afterward. A $1B peak gives me $6.35 per share. This uses one sales path rather than the three weighted paths in my main estimate.

That is why I do not think “it can reach $1B in sales” settles the valuation. I still need to know the margins, how long sales last, the pipeline contribution, and how many shares there will be. Under the adverse-safety assumptions, reaching $8.48 would require about $1.73B in peak core sales.

There is another way to get there. My optimistic operating case gives $8.27 at a 9% discount rate and $8.85 at 8%. So someone who expects better execution and accepts a lower return can reasonably get a higher value than I do. Those are the assumptions I would want to discuss.

The $1.50B figure depends on the rest of my inputs. It is what my calculation needs, rather than a claim about what every investor expects.

The conversion still has to be earned

Start with progression after prior therapy, then establish tumor-procurement feasibility, fitness for lymphodepletion and IL-2, referral, financial clearance, manufacturing success, and completed infusion. I have not assigned unsupported percentages to these steps. Before treating $1B or $1.5B as achievable, these conversion rates and realized net revenue need evidence. The 7.5% trial mortality figure is regimen-level safety context, not a number to transfer directly into this funnel.

How much confidence I put in the number

I would not put much weight on the second decimal in $5.83. Sales, margins, trial outcomes, and dilution can each change the result. I use the number to make my assumptions clear and to see which ones matter most.

In 200,000 simulations with independent program outcomes, I get a median of $5.69 and a 10th–90th percentile range of $2.17–$9.50. About 17% of the simulated values are above $8.48. That percentage depends on the distributions I chose; I would not read it as a 17% chance of the stock going up.

I also tested whether the programs might succeed or fail together. Using a shared latent-normal factor of 0.5 widens the range to $1.92–$9.78, with a $5.66 median and about 18% above $8.48. That 0.5 is a simulation setting, not a measured correlation between approvals.

How the simulation is constructed

Seed 11; 200,000 draws. Core value uses a lognormal revenue multiplier (log standard deviation 0.32) scaled to preserve the expected core PV, plus a normal margin shift with 5 percentage points standard deviation. Program success is binary, with probabilities from the model. Conditional peak sales are lognormal, with log standard deviation 0.5 for frontline and 0.6 for other programs. The same frontline event drives the label-withdrawal branch. Development spending, net cash, corporate costs, and the next-generation option remain fixed. The model omits many real-world dependencies and financing paths.

There are also parts I have simplified. I use revenue times margin as a rough measure of cash flow, rather than a full forecast of capital spending and working capital. Taxes, restricted cash, and future financing need more work. I hold the share count at 475M and stop the cash flows in 2045. I would revisit those choices before treating this as a fresh valuation at a later date.

Written 1 November 2026 · before the scheduled Q3 report

The update rule, before the results.

Iovance scheduled its Q3 report for 5 November 2026 at 8:30 a.m. ET. The original $410–420M guidance and $170.743M H1 revenue imply an H2 quarterly average of $119.6285–124.6285M. This is an average, not a promise that each quarter falls in the range.

  1. Revenue below $119.63M: calculate the Q4 revenue now needed to meet guidance; investigate treatment timing, capacity and demand before revising the 2027 ramp.
  2. Revenue within $119.63–124.63M: compare the remaining Q4 requirement with the company’s explanation, gross margin, and cash use. Being on the implied average alone does not validate $1B peak sales.
  3. Revenue above $124.63M: stronger quarterly execution supports reconsidering the ramp only if treatment throughput, margins and guidance also support it.
  4. Cash or share-count changes: replace the dated estimates together; financing adds cash and can dilute shares. Separate that mechanical update from a new clinical probability.
  5. Clinical/regulatory disclosures: review ORR, PFS/confirmation, durability, regimen-specific safety and prospective subgroup evidence separately. Do not turn a response-rate interval into a probability of approval.

There is no automatic price-target revision from one quarterly revenue number. Each subsequent version will record the old input, new input, dated evidence, and value impact. The historical $5.83 remains the 30 October snapshot.

At an illustrative $122.5M Q3, Q4 would need $116.757–126.757M to meet the original full-year guidance.

What would change my mind?

  • More patients treated, with better margins. I want to see revenue meet the roughly $120–125M H2 quarterly average implied by guidance. Sustained growth and improving margins would give me a reason to raise the melanoma assumptions.
  • An updated cash and share count. I need to replace my $240M and 475M estimates with the latest numbers. More cash helps, but I also need to account for any shares issued to raise it.
  • Clearer lung data. I want to see how long responses last, the full safety results, and outcomes by lymphodepletion regimen. If the selected subgroup performs well prospectively, I would have more reason to raise the lung probability.
  • Better evidence from TILVANCE-301. That could raise frontline value and reduce my concern about the existing label. Disappointing results would push me the other way.
  • Evidence of how competition affects demand. If Iovance keeps growing despite more treatment options, I would be more comfortable giving the stronger sales paths more weight.

I am open to changing the $5.83. What I want is a reason to change a particular assumption. If the evidence improves, the valuation should improve with it.
